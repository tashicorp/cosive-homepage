How the ASD June 2026 ISM Uplifts CTI Programs

Prescott Pym
Principal Security Advisor
18 Aug 2026 8 min read
Table of Contents

  2. Implications for CTI programs

  6. Conclusion

The Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) released the June 2026 update to the Information Security Manual (ISM) on 2 June 2026. This update includes 14 new controls and revisions to 23 existing controls, with several directly impacting how organisations structure and operate their cyber threat intelligence (CTI) programs.

For security teams already running CTI programs, these changes represent both validation of existing practices and a push toward more formalised, measurable intelligence operations. For those still building capability, the updated ISM provides a clearer roadmap than ever before.

What changed in the June 2026 ISM

The most significant additions fall into three categories: intelligence sharing requirements, indicator lifecycle management, and reporting obligations. Here is a summary of the key new and revised controls:

  • ISM-1823: Organisations must establish formal intelligence-sharing agreements with at least one sector-specific sharing community
  • ISM-1824: Threat intelligence indicators must be reviewed and validated at least every 90 days
  • ISM-1825: CTI programs must document their collection requirements aligned to organisational risk profiles
  • ISM-0962 (revised): Expanded scope for threat and risk assessments to include intelligence-driven scenarios
The shift from 'nice to have' to 'must have' for intelligence sharing is the most consequential change in this ISM update. It formalises what the CTI community has advocated for years.

Implications for CTI programs

These controls signal a maturation of the Australian Government's expectations around cyber threat intelligence. Rather than treating CTI as an adjunct to security operations, the ISM now positions it as a foundational capability.

Sharing is no longer optional

ISM-1823 requires organisations to join or establish a formal sharing community. For many organisations already participating in ISACs or using platforms like MISP, this is business as usual. For others, it means evaluating sharing communities and potentially standing up new infrastructure.

The control specifically mentions sector-specific communities, which means generic threat feeds alone will not satisfy the requirement. Organisations need to demonstrate active participation in bidirectional intelligence sharing.

Indicator hygiene becomes mandatory

ISM-1824 introduces a 90-day review cycle for threat intelligence indicators. This addresses a common problem: stale indicators that generate false positives and erode trust in CTI feeds. Teams will need to implement processes for:

  1. Tracking indicator age and provenance
  2. Re-validating indicators against current threat landscape
  3. Deprecating indicators that no longer represent active threats
  4. Documenting the review process for audit purposes

Aligning with CTI-CMM maturity levels

The new ISM controls map well to the CTI Capability Maturity Model (CTI-CMM). Organisations at Level 2 (Developing) should be able to meet most of the new requirements with process improvements. Those at Level 1 (Initial) will need more significant investment.

# Example: Mapping ISM controls to CTI-CMM levels
ISM-1823 (Sharing agreements)    → CTI-CMM Level 2: Developing
ISM-1824 (Indicator review)      → CTI-CMM Level 2: Developing
ISM-1825 (Collection reqs)       → CTI-CMM Level 3: Defined
ISM-0962 (Intel-driven TRAs)     → CTI-CMM Level 3: Defined

Practical steps for security teams

If your organisation needs to uplift its CTI program to meet the new ISM controls, here are the most impactful steps to take in the first 90 days:

1. Audit your current sharing arrangements

Document which sharing communities you participate in, the cadence of your contributions, and whether the agreements are formalised. If you are consuming threat intelligence without contributing back, this is the gap to address first.

2. Implement indicator lifecycle tracking

Set up a review schedule for your indicator database. MISP's built-in decay scoring system can automate much of this, but you still need human review for context and accuracy.

3. Document collection requirements

Work with your risk and governance teams to align CTI collection priorities with the organisational risk register. These should be living documents, reviewed quarterly at minimum.

Tooling considerations

Platforms like MISP are well-positioned to support these requirements out of the box. The platform's sharing group model directly supports ISM-1823, while its correlation engine and decay scoring address ISM-1824. For organisations not yet running MISP, this may be the catalyst to evaluate it seriously.

Conclusion

The June 2026 ISM update is a significant step forward for CTI in Australia. It codifies practices that leading organisations have followed for years and creates clear expectations for those catching up. The transition period gives teams time to build capability, but the direction is unambiguous: intelligence-driven security is now the baseline, not the aspiration.
